NDAA-Compliant Cameras: What Defense Contractors in Alabama Need to Know

BF

Barry Fuller

Owner / Operator — Overwatch Systems Integrated

May 28, 2026 8 min read
Share:

Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act is not ambiguous. Federal contractors and subcontractors cannot use telecommunications or video surveillance equipment from five specific manufacturers: Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision, and Dahua. That prohibition extends to subsidiaries, affiliates, and any product that contains covered components, even if the end-product brand does not appear on the restricted list.

Most procurement teams in Alabama know the five names. Fewer know the full scope of the rule or how enforcement has expanded since 2020. If your facility holds a federal contract, or supports a prime contractor who does, your surveillance infrastructure is subject to audit.

What the Law Actually Covers

Part A of Section 889 (effective August 2019) prohibits agencies from buying covered equipment directly. Part B (effective August 2020) is where most facilities run into problems: it prohibits doing business with any entity that uses covered equipment, regardless of whether that equipment has anything to do with the government contract in question.

In plain terms: if your facility uses a Hikvision camera to monitor your parking lot, and that camera is connected to any network, you may be in violation of a federal acquisition regulation. The equipment does not need to be on the contract. It does not need to be visible to federal personnel. The prohibition is on the use of covered equipment by covered contractors.

Key Scope Detail

The FAR rule at 52.204-25 requires contractors to represent, before each contract award, that they do not use covered technology. A false representation is a False Claims Act exposure, not just a contract compliance issue. The stakes are higher than most facilities realize.

The rulemaking also extended to covered services, meaning a vendor who provides managed video surveillance using prohibited hardware is also a problem for your compliance posture, even if the equipment is off-site and the service is delivered remotely.

The Supply Chain Problem

The five manufacturers named in Section 889 collectively hold a large share of the global IP camera component market. Many brands that appear compliant at the product label level contain image sensors, chipsets, or firmware from restricted manufacturers. A camera sold under a European brand name may use Dahua hardware internally.

This is not a hypothetical edge case. The Department of Defense has issued guidance explicitly warning that section 889 compliance cannot be based on brand name alone. Component-level verification is required.

"Knowing your vendor is not the same as knowing your supply chain. The manufacturers most commonly found embedded in third-party branded cameras are exactly the ones the law restricts."

Barry Fuller, Overwatch Systems Integrated

Axis Communications, based in Sweden and an authorized channel partner of OSI, maintains publicly available supply chain documentation confirming that their products do not contain components from the five restricted manufacturers. That documentation is auditable, which matters when contracting officers ask for it.

What NDAA Compliance Actually Requires

Compliance is not a checkbox. It requires three things:

A current inventory of all connected surveillance equipment. This includes cameras, video recorders, network switches used exclusively for camera traffic, and any IoT device with a lens or microphone. Facilities with legacy systems frequently discover equipment they did not know existed during this process.

Documentation of the supply chain for each device. Manufacturer compliance letters are a starting point, not a finish line. Axis, for instance, provides NDAA compliance statements specific to each product line. That documentation needs to be retained as part of your contract compliance record.

A remediation plan for non-compliant equipment. If covered equipment is found, it needs a replacement timeline and interim risk controls. Swapping hardware without validating the replacement's supply chain only defers the problem.

FIPS 140-2 Is a Separate Requirement

Some federal contracts also require FIPS 140-2 validated encryption for video data at rest and in transit. NDAA compliance and FIPS compliance are distinct: a camera can be NDAA-compliant and still fail FIPS requirements, or vice versa. Axis publishes FIPS status for each relevant model. Confirm both before specifying a camera for a government-adjacent deployment.

Common Mistakes OSI Sees in Northeast Alabama

The most frequent compliance gap we find is legacy equipment installed before the rule took effect. Facilities built out their surveillance infrastructure in 2015 to 2018 using whatever the integrator specified at the time. Many of those cameras came from manufacturers that are now restricted or used restricted components. The hardware is still running. Nobody looked at it after installation.

The second most common issue is facilities that replaced their NVR or VMS software but kept the old cameras. The front-end looks modern. The cameras sending video to it are not compliant. Auditors are not looking at your software interface. They are looking at what hardware is on the network.

Third: facilities that assumed their IT department had addressed the issue because IT replaced network switches. Surveillance cameras sit on their own VLANs in most properly designed systems, and IT teams often do not manage the cameras themselves. The camera inventory and the network equipment inventory are different documents. Both need to be current.

What the Audit Process Looks Like

Contracting officers typically do not audit every piece of equipment in person. What they do is require a contractor certification at award and at renewal. That certification is a legal representation. If it is wrong, the consequences are not limited to losing the contract.

OSI can perform a facility walkthrough and produce a documented inventory of surveillance equipment with compliance status for each device. That documentation supports your certification and gives your legal and compliance team something to work with if questions arise later. The walkthrough is no-obligation. For facilities with existing contracts and upcoming renewals, the timing matters.

Recommended Path Forward

Start with a physical inventory, not a software scan. Network discovery tools miss cameras on isolated VLANs and miss analog cameras connected through hybrid DVRs. A physical walk of the facility with a clipboard is faster and more accurate than trying to enumerate devices from a network management console.

Once you have a list, pull the manufacturer's compliance documentation for each model. If documentation does not exist, that model should be considered non-compliant for purposes of planning until you can prove otherwise.

Replace non-compliant equipment on a risk-ranked schedule. Start with cameras on networks that touch federal systems, then work outward. An Axis camera replacing a restricted-manufacturer device typically carries a three to five year lifecycle that outlasts most contract periods, so the replacement cost amortizes well.
