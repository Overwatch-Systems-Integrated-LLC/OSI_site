Alabama's $20M Industrial Site Investment: What It Means for Security Infrastructure

BF

Barry Fuller

Owner / Operator — Overwatch Systems Integrated

June 25, 2026 3 min read
Share:

Governor Kay Ivey announced more than $20 million in Growing Alabama Tax Credit awards this May, directing funding to 10 economic development organizations across the state. The goal is straightforward: make Alabama's industrial sites ready for new investment before a manufacturer ever breaks ground.

The awards span the state. The Shoals Economic Development Authority received $4.5 million for work at Shoals Research Airpark. The Industrial Development Board of Montgomery received $3.6 million. The Industrial Development Board of the City of Albertville received $3.2 million. The Dothan-Houston County Airport Authority received $2.2 million. More than 29 percent of this funding round was directed at Targeted Counties, reflecting the administration's continued push to bring industrial development to rural Alabama communities that have historically had limited access to job-ready infrastructure.

The Growing Alabama Tax Credit program uses private dollars to improve publicly owned sites. When a site clears this process, it signals to prospective manufacturers that the core infrastructure layer - roads, utilities, broadband - is already in place. Site selectors and corporate real estate teams use certified, job-ready sites to compress the timeline between decision and groundbreaking. For Alabama, that means more manufacturing jobs arriving faster.

What that program does not address is the security and monitoring layer. That is where facilities consistently fall short.

New manufacturing facilities make a predictable mistake during build-out. Security gets sequenced last. Network infrastructure goes in, production equipment arrives, commissioning runs, and then someone calls a commercial alarm company for a camera at the front entrance and a motion sensor on the back door. The result is a system that wasn't designed for the environment, doesn't integrate with operations, and gives management little visibility into what's actually happening on the floor, at the perimeter, or in the yard at 2 AM.

Industrial surveillance requires planning before the first conduit goes in the wall. Camera placement has to account for sight lines, lighting at shift change, environmental conditions - dust, heat, vibration, humidity - and network topology across a floor plan that may cover several hundred thousand square feet. An access control installation for a manufacturing facility is fundamentally different from one for a business park. Perimeter coverage at a receiving dock where trucks arrive around the clock requires different optics, different mounting, and different analytics than coverage of an interior corridor.

The right time to design security infrastructure is during site planning, not after production equipment is already installed. Retrofitting a camera system into a facility that wasn't designed for it costs more, covers less, and produces footage that's harder to use when an incident actually happens.
