A $500 Million Reminder: North Alabama's Industrial Growth Needs Surveillance Infrastructure Designed In, Not Bolted On

BF

Barry Fuller

Owner / Operator — Overwatch Systems Integrated

July 13, 2026 5 min read
Share:

Walk a half-finished 500,000-square-foot building site two months before the doors close up, and you'll see the same thing every time. Conduit runs through walls that will be sealed by fall. Electricians are pulling cable on a schedule set by the general contractor, not by whoever eventually has to secure the place. Camera and network planning shows up on someone's list near the bottom, usually after the equipment is already running and the plant manager is asking why the loading dock still doesn't have coverage.

That gap is normal. It's also expensive to fix later, and it's about to become a much bigger issue in North Alabama than it has been.

A $500 million data point

In April 2026, JST Corp., the Japan-based electronic connector manufacturer, announced a $500 million expansion at Conners Island Business Park in Guntersville, Alabama. The project puts a 540,000-square-foot facility on 240 acres next to Lake Guntersville, plus a separate 10,000-square-foot sales, marketing, and engineering building. It adds 80 new jobs to the 25 JST already employs in Guntersville, where the company has had a small presence since 2003. Production is described as heavily robotic: injection molding, stamping, plating, and connector and cable assembly running largely automated.

To be clear, this article uses the JST expansion as market context, not as an OSI project. OSI has no role in that facility and no relationship with JST. What the announcement does show is the scale of industrial investment landing in this part of the state right now, and JST is one project among several. Large, automation-heavy manufacturing footprints are becoming a normal feature of the North Alabama landscape, not an exception.

Automated floors change what "coverage" means

A facility built around robotic production lines has fewer people walking the floor at any given time than a traditional plant of the same size. That's the point of automating it. It also means fewer human eyes catching the small stuff: a jammed feed line, an unauthorized door propped open near a receiving dock, a contractor in an area they shouldn't be in during a multi-year buildout phase. Video coverage and the network that carries it stop being a security line item and start being an operational one, on the same level as the process equipment itself.

The mistake most new industrial buildings make isn't skipping surveillance. It's treating it as an add-on scoped after construction is substantially done, sourced from whichever installer happens to be available, on whatever network segment is left over once the operational technology gets its bandwidth. That approach produces exactly the kind of system OSI gets called to fix two or three years later: cameras on the wrong VLAN, no as-built documentation, a support line that takes a week to call back.

Why a local integrator matters more, not less, as facilities get bigger

The instinct with a project this size is to assume it needs a national vendor. It's the opposite. A build of that scale, on a compressed construction schedule, benefits from an integrator who can walk the site in person on short notice, adjust camera placement as the floor plan firms up, and hand over documentation the facility's own team can maintain without calling back every time something changes.

Site assessment, camera placement engineered around actual coverage and blind spots, and network design built for surveillance workloads from the start, not squeezed onto a network sized for something else. Every project ships with full documentation so the system can be maintained and expanded without OSI in the room. What this means if you're planning a facility, expansion, or retrofit

If your organization has a new build, an expansion, or an older system that hasn't kept pace with a facility that's grown around it, the right time to plan surveillance and network infrastructure is during design, not after occupancy. That's true whether the project is a fraction of JST's scale or many times larger.
